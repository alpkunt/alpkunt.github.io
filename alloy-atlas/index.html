Composition Cartography · A personal alloy atlas

Every alloy is a recipe written in elements.

Strip an alloy down and what is left is a short list of elements and their weights. Stainless steel is iron with a fifth of it swapped for chromium; bronze is copper with a twentieth tin. This atlas reads each alloy as a thirty-one-element recipe and measures which recipes are really alike. Pick one — the periodic table lights up with what is inside it.

02

The systems the compositions drew by themselves

Nobody labelled these. A k‑means clustering of the recipes splits the set into eight groups, and they land almost exactly on the families metallurgists already use: the iron base, the nickel–chromium superalloys, the copper base, the light metals, the low‑melting solders, the refractory mixes. One group comes back with a single member — that is not a failure but the clustering pointing out that this set holds exactly one zinc‑base alloy.

03

Which elements travel together

Element × element · overlap in presence, not in quantity

How to read a cell. Of the alloys that contain either of the two elements, what share contain both. Presence only — carbon at 0.08% counts exactly like iron at 99%, because the question here is who turns up together, not in what proportion. A mass-weighted version of this chart was tried first and was useless: the base metals swamped it and 93% of the matrix collapsed into a single shade.

Why the elements are in this order. Not by atomic number — that scatters partners across the grid. They are seriated by their own position in this co‑occurrence space, so elements that travel together sit side by side and the blocks become visible.

The steel‑making block runs along one corner: iron with manganese at 63%, with silicon at 58%, with chromium at 50%, with nickel at 49%. Chromium and nickel reach 48% — that pairing is stainless. Aluminium and magnesium at 47% are the light‑alloy corner. But the strongest bond in the whole matrix, hafnium with tantalum at 67%, deserves suspicion rather than excitement: it rests on three alloys. Among rare elements a single shared recipe moves the number a long way, so read the bright cells in the sparse corner as a hint, not a finding.

04

Every pair, at once

Recipe affinity ordered by system — bright blocks are the families

How to read this. Each alloy is a vector of thirty-one element weights summing to 100. Because those weights are parts of a whole, ordinary distance would mislead — raise the chromium and the iron must fall whether the metallurgist meant it or not. Weights are therefore square‑root transformed, which turns each recipe into a point on a sphere where straight‑line distance is a fair measure of being made of the same stuff. Affinity is a Gaussian function of that distance, 100 meaning an identical recipe.

Where the numbers come from. Specifications give ranges, not numbers. Range‑specified elements use the midpoint; elements given only as a maximum use the maximum, which biases minor elements upward and the base metal downward. Every row states which. Full rules and every source are on the Sources & Method tab.

Two recipes can share almost no elements and still behave alike, and two that look nearly identical can sit on opposite sides of a phase boundary. So each composition goes through nine descriptors metallurgists use to predict behaviour. None of these are new measurements — they are all arithmetic on the same table.

05

Where the two atlases disagree

Pairs whose recipe rank and behaviour rank pull hardest in opposite directions

A pair high on this list is either a convergence — different elements arriving at the same physics — or a near‑miss, where a small change of recipe moves the alloy somewhere else entirely. Both are the interesting cases, and a single blended similarity score would have averaged them into invisibility.

How the descriptors are built. Weight percents become mole fractions, then: mixing entropy is −Σx·ln x in units of R, peaking at ln N for an equal‑parts mix; size mismatch and electronegativity spread are the composition‑weighted scatter of atomic radius and Pauling electronegativity; VEC is the average valence electron count. Density follows the inverse rule of mixtures, melting point and cost the direct one.

What these are not. First‑order screening rules, the kind used to decide what is worth trying — not a phase diagram, and no substitute for CALPHAD or a furnace. The rule of mixtures ignores ordering, precipitation and porosity, and the descriptors say nothing about heat treatment, which routinely matters more than composition.

Sources & Method

Every row, and where it came from.

This tab exists because the first version of this dataset was assembled from recall rather than from sources, and could not be defended. What follows is the correction: the tier each row sits in, the designation it claims, whether its specification has actually been opened, and the rule used to turn a range into a number.

The three tiers
  1. A — standard · a published standard specifies the chemistry, not merely a mechanical class. UNS, ASTM, AA, SAE, AMS, EN.
  2. B — literature · no standard exists; a peer‑reviewed paper defines the composition. The research alloys.
  3. C — nominal · trade or generic name, composition from one named supplier datasheet. The weakest rows, and marked as such.

A row also carries READ or candidate. That is a separate axis from the tier: it records whether the source was actually retrieved, not how good the source is.

Turning a spec into one number
  1. R — range · e.g. Cr 18.0–20.0 → the midpoint, 19.0. No bias.
  2. M — maximum only · e.g. Mn ≤ 2.0 → the maximum. Biases minor elements upward.
  3. B — balance · 100 minus the rest. Absorbs the bias above.
  4. E — exact · fixed by definition, as in an equiatomic alloy.

The maximum was chosen for M because it is reproducible from the standard alone. Half the maximum is arbitrary; a typical mill analysis needs its own citation per element per grade and mostly does not exist in published form. The cost is a known, one‑directional bias: read minor elements as upper bounds and base metals as lower bounds.

One test this atlas can fail, and one it cannot. Density predicted from the recipe by the rule of mixtures tracks published handbook density closely. That is an internal consistency check — it confirms the compositions and the element constants agree with each other, and an invented composition would likely break it. It does not confirm that a row matches its specification. Only the citation in the row does that, which is why the READ badge is the number that matters on this page.

08

The nine derived descriptors

Every number below is computed from the table above — nothing new is added

What was left out. Nitrogen, sulphur, boron and the rare earths appear in real specifications but are dropped to keep the element set legible; so are residuals below roughly 0.05%. Phosphorus was originally dropped too, until the C51000 specification made the omission absurd — a phosphor bronze with no phosphorus — so it was restored. The remaining absences matter most for nitrogen‑strengthened duplex grades and the magnesium alloys, where hundredths of a percent change the metal.

Element constants. Atomic mass, metallic radius, Pauling electronegativity, valence electron count, density and melting point are textbook values. Prices are order‑of‑magnitude figures for the pure element and move with the market, so the cost descriptor is a ranking, not a quote.

Signature Element

The one element that makes it what it is.

Not the biggest ingredient — that is almost always the boring answer, and it is iron nine times out of ten. The signature element is the one this alloy holds far more of than alloys in general do, found the way a search engine finds the word that defines a document: weight against rarity.

How the lift is calculated

For every element in a recipe, divide its weight percent by that element’s average weight percent across the whole set. An element sitting at three times its usual share is doing three times its usual work. The highest ratio wins.

The measure is deliberately blunt and its failures are instructive. It hands mild steel iron, which is right — plain carbon steel really is just iron. It hands the single‑crystal turbine alloy rhenium at three percent, which is also right, and is most of the reason that alloy costs what it does. Where it goes wrong is on alloys defined by something they do not contain: lead‑free solder is remarkable for the lead that was taken out, and no ratio over what is present can see an absence.

Every alloy

Tap any to see its signature

Why lift and not the largest share. Ranking by raw weight percent would return the base metal for almost every alloy and tell you nothing. Dividing by each element’s average share across the set asks a better question: what is unusual here? It is the arithmetic behind TF‑IDF in text search, applied to metal instead of words. Elements below 0.3% are ignored so trace residuals cannot win on rarity alone.
